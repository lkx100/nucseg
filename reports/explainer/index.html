    Part 1

    The job: colour in every nucleus

    The model gets a microscope photo. For every pixel it must say whether that pixel belongs to a cell nucleus. An expert has already drawn the right answer for each photo. On this page we call that drawing the answer key.

    1. This is one of our photos. The gray blobs are nuclei.
    2. An expert drew this answer key. White means nucleus. Papers call it the ground-truth mask.
    3. This is what our model drew for the same photo. At first sight it looks the same.
    4. Laid on top of each other, the two drawings differ only in thin rims at the edges of the nuclei. Red is where the model marked too much. Blue is where it marked too little.
    5. The Dice score turns that into one number between 0 and 1. Count the pixels both drawings share, double that, and divide by the pixels of both drawings added up. This photo scores 0.961.
    6. The same model on a stained tissue photo scores 0.807. The nuclei touch each other and the colours vary, so this type is harder.

    Four kinds of photo

    The 603 photos we score come from four kinds of microscope picture. In fluorescence photos the nuclei glow on a dark background. In H&E photos the tissue is stained pink and purple. Brightfield photos are plain light-microscope pictures. Hover over a photo, or tap it, to see its answer key.

    The final score of a run is an average over all 603 photo scores. Part 3 gives the exact rule. Every photo counts the same, whether it holds 3 nuclei or 300.

    Keep two things in mind for later. The model's mistakes are thin rims at nucleus edges. And the final score is an average over photos, so a few very bad photos can pull it down.

    Part 2

    The model: zoom out to understand, zoom back in to draw

    Our model is called a Swin-T U-Net. Swin-T is a small transformer, and U-Net is the U shape you will see below. The name describes two halves. The first half shrinks the photo step by step to work out what is in it. The second half grows it back to full size to draw the mask.

    1. The photo enters as a crop of 256 × 256 pixels.
    2. First the model cuts the crop into patches of 4 × 4 pixels. Each patch becomes one cell of a 64 × 64 grid. From here on, the model no longer sees single pixels.
    3. Four Swin stages follow. Each one halves the grid: 64, 32, 16, then 8 cells across. Inside a stage, nearby cells exchange information. That exchange is the "attention" of a transformer. It lets each cell learn about its surroundings.
    4. At the bottom, one cell covers 32 × 32 pixels of the photo. The model now knows well what is in the photo, but only roughly where. This half is the encoder. It arrives already trained on about 1.3 million everyday photos (ImageNet), so it does not start from zero.
    5. The second half, the decoder, climbs back up: 16, 32, 64 cells across. At each step it doubles the grid and receives a copy of the encoder's grid of the same size. These copies are the skip connections. They bring back the "where" that shrinking lost.
    6. Two more doublings reach 128 and then 256 cells across, the size of the photo. A last layer turns every cell into one number, the probability that this pixel is nucleus. Above 0.5 counts as nucleus.
    7. Here is the weak spot. The finest grid the encoder passes across has 64 × 64 cells. The last two doublings must guess the exact edge of every nucleus from cells that each cover 4 × 4 pixels.
    8. Run C fixed that. A small extra branch, shown in yellow, looks at the raw pixels at full size and at half size and hands them to the last two steps. It adds 28 thousand parameters to a model of 31.7 million. It is the one change that passed our win rule: the score rose from 0.9143 to 0.9196.

    When this page says run C or C*, it means this model with the yellow branch. Every experiment after C keeps it.

    Part 3

    How we test: every photo is examined once, by a model that never saw it

    A score only means something if the model is graded on photos it did not learn from. We also need to know how much a score moves by luck alone. This part shows both.

    Training photos Stop set (decides when training stops) Exam fold (papers say validation fold) Locked test set A pale orange dot has already been examined.
    1. We have 670 photos, each with an answer key.
    2. 67 of them go into a locked box, the test set. No run has ever touched them. We open the box only when a model reaches the target on all the other photos.
    3. The other 603 are dealt into 5 groups of about 120. The groups are called folds.
    4. Fold 0's turn. Fold 0 is the exam. A fresh model trains on 433 photos from the other four folds. 49 more are held aside as the stop set. After every pass over the training photos (an epoch) we score the stop set, and we keep the model from the epoch where that score was best.
    5. Then the exam. The model marks the 121 photos of fold 0, which it never saw. Each photo gets its own Dice score.
    6. The same happens four more times. Each time a fresh model trains, and a different fold is the exam.
    7. Now all 603 photos have a score, each from a model that did not train on it. We average each fold, then average the five fold scores. That number is the CV Dice (CV stands for cross-validation). It is the score we report for a run.

    How much is luck?

    1. We trained the first model twice with nothing changed. The first run scored 0.9178. The second scored 0.9143.
    2. That is a difference of 0.35 points from luck alone: which photos land together in a batch, and at which epoch training stops. So when a new idea moves the score by 0.2 points, we have learned nothing.
    3. So we fix a win rule before each run. A new idea wins only if it beats the baseline by at least 0.5 points and scores at least as high on 4 of the 5 folds.
    4. Run C passed. It beat its baseline by 0.53 points and was ahead on 4 folds. The run of experiment 08 did not. It was ahead on 4 folds, but by only 0.06 points.

    One trick at exam time

    At exam time the model can mark each photo in 8 orientations (4 rotations, each also mirrored) and average its 8 answers. This is called test-time augmentation, or TTA. It adds 0.05 to 0.16 points in our runs. Our best score, 0.9235, includes it.

    8 views of the same photo

    Two things from this part come back in part 5. The stop set is a group of 49 photos that decides when training stops. And two runs of the same thing can differ by 0.35 points, so smaller differences mean nothing.

    Part 4

    Eight experiments, one win

    Each experiment changes one thing and asks whether the score moves by more than luck. Step through them in the order we ran them.

    1. Experiment 01, run A. We start with the plain Swin-T U-Net from part 2, without the yellow branch. It scores 0.9178. This is the baseline.
    2. Run B: adversarial training. This is the idea behind NSL (neural structured learning). For every photo the model also trains on a copy with a tiny change, invisible to the eye, chosen to fool the model as much as possible. Score 0.9144, lower than the baseline on all 5 folds. It did make the model hard to fool: under such an attack its score stays at 0.918, where the baseline drops to 0.800. Training took 2.4 times as long.
    3. Experiment 02 first reran the baseline with nothing changed: 0.9143. The 0.35-point gap to the first run is our measure of luck, drawn as the shaded band.
    4. Run C: the full-size branch. This is the yellow branch from part 2. Score 0.9196, which is 0.53 points over the rerun and ahead on 4 of 5 folds. It passes the win rule. From here on, C is the model.
    5. Runs D and G: enlarged photos. We fed the model photos enlarged to twice the size, so each nucleus covers more grid cells. Without the yellow branch that gave 0.9190, with it 0.9196. No gain over C, at about 3 times the training time.
    6. Run E: adversarial training again, on top of C, with a change half as large. 0.9197. The model was again harder to fool (0.905 under attack against 0.818), and again the score did not move. We closed this idea for the Dice target.
    7. Run F: altered copies. The model trained on photos that were warped, recoloured, blurred and speckled with noise. 0.9213. That is less than 0.2 points over C, inside the luck band.
    8. Experiment 06: train longer. Training often stopped early, sometimes before epoch 30 of 60. We forbade stopping before epoch 30. C became C* at 0.9207, our baseline since then. F became F* at 0.9201. With training length equal, the altered copies added nothing.
    9. Experiment 07: average finished models. Nothing was trained. We averaged the answers of models we already had. Two models gave 0.9217. Four gave 0.9230. With the 8-orientation trick from part 3 that is 0.9235, our best score.
    10. Experiment 08: make edges count more. 83% of all wrong pixels lie within 2 pixels of a nucleus edge. So during training, mistakes near an edge counted 5 times as much. 0.9213. No gain.
    11. Step back. From run C on, eight single-model runs fit inside one luck band. Five different training ideas gave the same score. That tells us the limit is not in how we train. Part 5 looks at where it is.

    One change to the model worked. Changes to the training did not. That is why the next part stops looking at runs and starts looking at single photos.

    Part 5

    Where the missing points are

    So far we looked at one number per run. This part opens that number up into the 603 photo scores behind it. All numbers here come from our best result, the average of four models.

    1. Each square is one photo, placed by its score. There are 603 of them, the worst on the left and the best on the right.
    2. The photo in the middle, the median, scores 0.938. Half of all photos score higher. The target is 0.937. So the typical photo is already there.
    3. But a run is graded by its average, and the average is 0.9235. A few very low scores pull it down, the way one zero pulls down a class average.
    4. 47 photos score below 0.85. That is 8% of the photos. Together they cost 1.46 points, more than the whole 1.35-point gap to the target.
    5. This is not bad luck on the day. When we average only two models instead of four, 44 of the same 47 photos are below 0.85 again. The model fails on particular photos.
    6. Three squares sit far to the left, at 0.00, 0.01 and 0.36. The next picture is about them.

    One wrong answer key

    We did not save the model's drawings for these photos. We know their size from the error counts: on photo A the wrong pixels cover 19% of the photo, and on photo B they cover as much as its whole answer key.

    Two photos out of 49 falling to zero would cost about 3.8 points, so the size fits. This link is my explanation, not something we measured. It would also explain why fold 1 stopped early in all three runs of experiment 02.

    1. Among the 603 photos we score, exactly three have this size and look. They are 347 pixels wide and 260 high, and show the same kind of bright nuclei on a dark background.
    2. Here are their answer keys. Two are fine. The key of photo A is almost empty: 26 marked pixels on a photo full of nuclei. This answer key is wrong.
    3. Fold 0's turn. Photo A is in the exam. The model learned from photos B and C, so it marks the nuclei. The empty key says there are none. Score: 0.000.
    4. Fold 4's turn. Now photos B and C are in the exam and photo A is in the training set. It is the only photo of this look the model studies, and its key says: nothing here.
    5. So the model marks almost nothing on photo B and only a small part of the nuclei on photo C. Scores: 0.008 and 0.364.
    6. These three photos cost 0.40 points of the 1.35 we are missing. Without them the average would be 0.9275.
    7. The same wrong key explains an old puzzle. Photos B and C also sit in the stop set of fold 1. In that round the model trains on the empty key. The chart shows our baseline run C*: fold 1's stop-set score settles about 3 points under the other folds.

    The rest of the low group

    These are the 24 lowest-scoring photos, the three from above included. Hover over one, or tap it, to see its answer key. Many are very dark, so there is a switch to brighten them.

    Of the 47 low photos, 32 are small fluorescence photos, 9 are stained tissue, 5 are large fluorescence photos and 1 is brightfield. Several are dark or out of focus. We have not yet looked at what the model drew on them, so we do not know why they fail.

    The gap is not spread thinly over all photos. It sits in a small group. One cause is a single wrong answer key, and it reaches three photos and one stop set.

    Part 6

    Where this leaves us

    Three points hold the whole project so far.

    1. One model change worked.
    Giving the model a full-size view of the pixels (run C) lifted the score from about 0.914 to 0.920.
    2. Nothing after it did.
    Nine more runs tried five training ideas and model averaging. All of them land between 0.919 and 0.923. Running the same thing twice already differs by 0.35 points.
    3. The missing points sit in few photos.
    The typical photo already scores 0.938. 47 of 603 photos pull the average down, and one wrong answer key explains three of them.
