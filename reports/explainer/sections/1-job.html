The job: colour in every nucleus

The model gets a microscope photo. For every pixel it must say whether that pixel belongs to a cell nucleus. An expert has already drawn the right answer for each photo. On this page we call that drawing the answer key.

  1. This is one of our photos. The gray blobs are nuclei.
  2. An expert drew this answer key. White means nucleus. Papers call it the ground-truth mask.
  3. This is what our model drew for the same photo. At first sight it looks the same.
  4. Laid on top of each other, the two drawings differ only in thin rims at the edges of the nuclei. Red is where the model marked too much. Blue is where it marked too little.
  5. The Dice score turns that into one number between 0 and 1. Count the pixels both drawings share, double that, and divide by the pixels of both drawings added up. This photo scores 0.961.
  6. The same model on a stained tissue photo scores 0.807. The nuclei touch each other and the colours vary, so this type is harder.

A rim one pixel wide costs a small nucleus far more than a large one. Most of our photos hold small nuclei.

Four kinds of photo

The 603 photos we score come from four kinds of microscope picture. In fluorescence photos the nuclei glow on a dark background. In H&E photos the tissue is stained pink and purple. Brightfield photos are plain light-microscope pictures. Hover over a photo, or tap it, to see its answer key.

The final score of a run is an average over all 603 photo scores. Part 3 gives the exact rule. Every photo counts the same, whether it holds 3 nuclei or 300.

Keep two things in mind for later. The model's mistakes are thin rims at nucleus edges. And the final score is an average over photos, so a few very bad photos can pull it down.
